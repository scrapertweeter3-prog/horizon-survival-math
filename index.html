A retirement plan that survives 30 years dies in year 31

Most withdrawal plans get tested against a 30-year table. A 45-year-old who stops at 75 does not have a 30-year problem, and neither does anyone planning on leaving money behind. Stretch the same plan five years and the verdict can flip from funded to broke with nothing else changed.

Take the common setup: $650,000 saved, $32,000 withdrawn the first year, withdrawals raised 3 percent a year, returns averaging 6.5 percent. Run the table for 30 years and the account still holds about $471,000. Declared funded. Stretch that identical plan to 35 years and it ends about $502,000 in the hole, having actually crossed zero in year 31. The 30-year pass was a finish-line artifact, not a verdict.

The average return is doing less work than it looks. At a smooth 5.5 percent, the same plan lasts 30 years. At 6.5, it lasts 38. One point of average return buys eight years of plan life, which is why any horizon longer than the table needs a survival check, not an ending-balance check.

Sequence makes the horizon problem worse. Put the bad years up front, minus 18, minus 8, minus 12 in years one through three, and the plan dies in year 9. Put the identical three bad years in years 18 through 20 instead and it survives to year 28. Same average return, same withdrawals, nineteen extra years from ordering alone. A plan that only had to reach year 30 never notices. A plan that needs year 45 finds out the hard way.

The cheapest lever is the starting rate. Drop the first withdrawal from $32,000 to $26,000, the 4 percent line on the same $650,000, and the 5.5 percent plan stretches from 30 years to 41. The 6.5 percent plan goes from 38 years to past 60. The first withdrawal sets the trajectory; every later one just follows it.

The method fits on an index card. Test the horizon you actually have, not the table everyone prints. Run one bad-sequence path against it, not just the average. Then check what one point less return and a few thousand off the first withdrawal do to the survival year. Ending balances answer the wrong question; the survival year answers the right one.
